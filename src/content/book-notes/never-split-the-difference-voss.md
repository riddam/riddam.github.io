Chris Voss's *Never Split the Difference* is a negotiation book built on the techniques he used as the FBI's lead international hostage negotiator — where there is no splitting the difference and no walking away. What makes it useful outside of hostage situations is that the same techniques apply to salary conversations, vendor pricing, and everyday disagreements.

*Originally published on Medium, 6 March 2025, lightly edited here.*

The core idea is a shift away from cold, logic-driven bargaining toward emotional intelligence. Instead of compromising to meet in the middle, Voss argues you get better outcomes by understanding the other party's emotions and guiding them toward a solution — one they feel they helped create.

## Tactical Empathy

Understand and acknowledge the emotions of the other party, then say them out loud. Voss calls this *labeling* — naming a feeling to defuse it: "It sounds like you're concerned about X." Labeling validates the other person without agreeing to anything, and it builds enough trust that they become more open to a solution.

## The Power of "No"

"No" is not the end of a negotiation — it's the beginning of a real one. A quick "yes" is often a way to end an uncomfortable conversation; a "no" makes the other person feel safe and in control, which is when they actually start telling you what matters to them. Stop chasing "yes" and start making it easy to say "no."

## Mirroring

Repeat the last one to three words the other person said, as a question, then go quiet. It sounds almost too simple, but it prompts them to elaborate without any sense of being pushed — and it creates a subconscious sense of connection that keeps them talking.

## Calibrated Questions

Open-ended "how" and "what" questions hand the other person a problem to solve on your behalf. They surface hidden constraints and motivations, and they slow the conversation down without confrontation.

> "What would need to happen for us to move forward?"

## The Illusion of Control

Let the other person feel like they are the one making the decisions. Questions that direct the conversation — without forcing an answer — keep them engaged and invested, because people defend a deal they helped build.

> "How can we structure this so it works for both of us?"

## The "That's Right" Moment

Aim to get the other person to say "that's right" — not "you're right", which is what people say to make you stop talking. "That's right" means you have summarized their perspective so accurately that they feel genuinely understood, and that is the moment real agreement becomes possible.

## The Accusation Audit

Name the objections before they can be raised against you. By saying the worst thing they might be thinking first, you take the sting out of it.

> "I know this might sound unfair, and you may think I'm being too rigid…"

## Applying It at Work

**Negotiating a raise.** Lead with tactical empathy — "I understand budgets are tight, and I respect the company's priorities" — then hand over the problem with a calibrated question: "What would need to happen for my salary to align with my contributions?" If they push back, mirror the last few words and let the silence do the work: "Align with my contributions?"

**Vendor pricing.** Mirroring keeps them talking: "This is the best price you can offer?" A calibrated question invites them to solve it: "How can we find efficiencies to meet both our goals?" And the illusion of control keeps them invested: "What options do you see that could work for both of us?"

**Handling discount requests.** Start with an accusation audit — "I know you might think I'm being inflexible, and I get that" — then a soft, reasoned "no": "I wish I could, but if I did, it would affect quality." Finish by redirecting to what *can* move: "What can we adjust in the scope to match your budget?"

## Final Takeaways

- Negotiation is information gathering, not just persuasion.
- Empathy is a superpower — the more people feel heard, the more flexible they become.
- Silence is an ally. Ask a great question, then pause.
- People fight for a deal they helped create. Use the illusion of control.

## Conclusion

Mastering negotiation isn't about winning at all costs — it's about understanding human behavior, building trust, and finding solutions that work for both sides. Whether you're negotiating a job offer, a business deal, or an everyday disagreement, tactical empathy and calibrated questions will get you further than any hard-line tactic. If you're serious about getting better at this, the book is well worth the read.

> "He who has learned to disagree without being disagreeable has discovered the most valuable secret of negotiation." — Chris Voss
