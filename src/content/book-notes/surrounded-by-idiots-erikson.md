> "The key to effective communication is understanding that not everyone thinks like you do." — Thomas Erikson

Have you ever been frustrated at work, wondering why your colleagues just don't get it? Maybe you feel surrounded by people who overcomplicate things, refuse to take action, or come across as too aggressive? Thomas Erikson's *Surrounded by Idiots* offers a simple but powerful framework for understanding different personality types and improving how you communicate at work.

*This is a book note I originally published [on Medium](https://medium.com/@riddamjain/surrounded-by-idiots-a-corporate-guide-to-understanding-personalities-75159277e42b) in March 2025, lightly edited here.*

## The Four Colors of Personality

Erikson sorts people into four types, each represented by a color:

- 🔴 **Red (Dominant, Decisive)** — Action-oriented, competitive, and direct. Can come across as aggressive or impatient.
- 🟡 **Yellow (Optimistic, Social)** — Enthusiastic, idea-driven, and outgoing. Can struggle with details and discipline.
- 🟢 **Green (Patient, Supportive)** — Calm, reliable, and team-oriented. Can resist change and avoid confrontation.
- 🔵 **Blue (Analytical, Precise)** — Logical, methodical, and data-driven. Can be overly critical and slow to decide.

Each type has strengths and weaknesses. The key to better collaboration isn't changing people — it's understanding and adapting to their communication style.

## DISC Profiling vs. Erikson's Colors

DISC profiling is widely used in corporate settings to assess behavioral styles. Erikson's color system maps closely onto DISC but presents it in a more relatable way:

| DISC | Color | In short |
|---|---|---|
| Dominance (D) | 🔴 Red | Direct, strong-willed, results-focused |
| Influence (I) | 🟡 Yellow | Social, enthusiastic, persuasive |
| Steadiness (S) | 🟢 Green | Cooperative, patient, reliable |
| Conscientiousness (C) | 🔵 Blue | Analytical, systematic, detail-focused |

Understanding both systems lets you apply personality insights more effectively in leadership, teamwork, and conflict resolution.

## How These Colors Show Up at Work

### 🔴 Red: the driven CEO who gets things done

Imagine your CEO is a Red. They want quick results, dislike unnecessary detail, and expect everyone to act fast. Presenting a project update? Don't drown them in spreadsheets — summarize the key takeaways, focus on business impact, and be direct.

> "A Red personality doesn't want a lengthy explanation. Get to the point, or risk losing their attention."

Be clear and concise, lead with action and results, and don't take their bluntness personally.

### 🟡 Yellow: the visionary who inspires

A marketing manager who is always full of new ideas, talks non-stop, and brings contagious enthusiasm to meetings. A classic Yellow: thrives on creativity, but may struggle with follow-through.

> "A Yellow loves brainstorming but might forget deadlines. Help them stay on track without dampening the enthusiasm."

Engage in open discussion and show excitement for their ideas — but don't rely on them for deep analysis or routine tasks.

### 🟢 Green: the dependable glue of the team

An HR manager who makes sure everyone feels heard, values harmony, and dislikes conflict. Greens prefer stability and can resist sudden change — so explain the benefits of a new workflow and reassure them.

> "Greens don't like confrontation. Push too hard and they'll disengage rather than push back."

Be patient and supportive, and give them time to process change — don't rush or pressure them to decide quickly.

### 🔵 Blue: the engineer who lives by logic

A lead engineer who insists on precise documentation and triple-checks the numbers. Blues prefer facts over feelings and value accuracy above all.

> "A Blue needs logic, not gut feelings. They won't accept decisions based on 'trust me on this.'"

Provide detailed, fact-based information and give them time to analyze — don't expect them to enjoy spontaneous brainstorming.

## Handling People with Mixed Colors

Most people aren't purely one color — they usually have a dominant color with a secondary influence:

- 🔴🟡 **Red-Yellow (energetic and visionary)** — bold and inspiring, but impatient. Give them room to lead, with accountability and big-picture goals.
- 🔴🔵 **Red-Blue (results-oriented and analytical)** — demanding but data-driven. Keep facts straight, lead with logical reasoning, skip the emotional detours.
- 🟡🟢 **Yellow-Green (friendly and supportive)** — great team players who can struggle to decide. Encourage initiative inside a collaborative, structured environment.
- 🔵🟢 **Blue-Green (thoughtful and loyal)** — reliable but change-averse. Pair logical explanations with reassurance to ease transitions.

The through-line: **meet people where they are.** Balance directness with reasoning, encourage strengths while quietly shoring up weaknesses, and blend your communication style to match theirs.

## Final Thoughts

Most workplaces are a mix of all four colors, and conflict arises when we fail to recognize different communication styles. Learn to approach each type on its own terms and you become a better leader, teammate, and communicator.

> "Understanding others is not about changing them, but about changing how you interact with them." — Thomas Erikson
